# The Hinge: Locke's Consent and Its Chief End

The work is two sentences spliced from John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* (1689) — the first from the chapter on the beginnings of political society, the second from the chapter on its ends — joined by an ellipsis and a "therefore." This is not a treatise but a hinge: the precise joint at which an account of human nature becomes a theory of legitimate rule. Read as presented, with its connectives cut, it performs as a self-grounding argument. That performance is both its power and the site of every question worth asking of it.

## The claim

The claim comes in three movements, each of a different logical kind. First, an anthropology stated as cosmic fact: "Men being… by nature all free, equal and independent." Second, a constraint drawn from that fact: "no one can be… subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent." Third, a purpose drawn from the constraint: "The great and chief end, therefore, of men's uniting into commonwealths, and putting themselves under government, is the preservation of their property." Metaphysics, deontology, teleology — three declaratives in apposition, welded by one connective.

The overall claim is a triple inversion of classical political philosophy. The individual is prior to the polity, not the polity to the individual (against Aristotle's polis as prior in nature). The end of politics is *preservation* — staying what one already is, minus inconvenience — not perfection or the cultivation of virtue. And authority is delegated, not given: rulers hold power on deposit, for an end set by those who deposited it. The gloss attached to the work names this rightly: legitimate power is fiduciary. A trustee holds title to nothing; breach of the trust dissolves the office. Everything that follows in the liberal tradition — limited government, constitutionalism, the right of revolution — is latent in that word "end."

## How it argues

It argues in the mode of geometry: premises stated as axioms, conclusion stated as theorem, no evidence anywhere. There is no history in the passage, no anthropology offered as observation, no argument for the opening clause at all — only a participle of fact, "Men being." The sentence's rhythm ("The great and chief end, therefore…") gives the air of a verdict; the method is assertion arranged to look like deduction.

The most telling feature is grammatical. The only actors in the passage are "men": they "unit[e] into commonwealths," they "put themselves under government." Power itself has no subject. "The political power of another" — another *whom*? The ruler never appears. The magistrate, the king, the sovereign are grammatically dissolved before the argument begins; power is an artifact men make and handle, not a person who wields it. This is the argument's politics performed in its syntax. Likewise the possessives: "his own consent," "their property." Ownership attaches to individuals throughout; the commonwealth possesses nothing, and government appears only as the place men go. A single pronoun — "their" — carries the entire anti-absolutist case: the property belongs to the subjects, not the polity, which means government preserves what it never owned, which means it is a bailee, which means it can be judged by the terms of the bailment.

That is the fiduciary core, and it is worth seeing how much work it does against the passage's actual polemical target. The *Treatises* were written against Filmer's defense of hereditary divine right. One clause — "no one can be… subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent" — rules out, in a single stroke, authority by birth, authority by conquest, and authority by divine grant to one man's line. If power is personal, only will can convey it; if only consent conveys it, then no majority, parliament, or army can manufacture a right that no individual possessed in the first place. What no one may do singly, none may claim by aggregation. This is the argument's genuine steel, and it is why the passage became the ur-text of popular sovereignty.

## The hinge word

The "therefore" is the passage's load-bearing beam, and it spans three gaps that are each filled by premises the work does not state.

From *nature* to *right*: that men are "by nature all free, equal and independent" yields the consent requirement only if nature is a normative order — if natural facts carry moral force. Locke's warrant is his natural-law theology (men as the workmanship of a Maker, hence not disposable by one another), entirely offstage here. Without it, the first sentence is description and the second is prohibition, and nothing connects them. Hobbes began from almost the same premise — natural liberty and equality — and derived absolute, unaccountable sovereignty. The identical anthropology supporting opposite conclusions is the surest sign that the premise underdetermines the inference; what differentiates Locke is the unstated background that makes liberty a moral property, inalienable by force.

From *right* to *consent*: the constraint follows only if liberty can be transferred by will alone — if what nature gives, only the owner's choice can title away. That inalienability thesis is the real center of Locke's politics, and it is invisible in the excerpt.

From *consent* to *end*: why would free and independent men ever consent to subjection? Only for gain — because the state of nature has "inconveniences" (no settled law, no indifferent judge, no power to enforce), because enjoyment of one's holdings is "very unsafe, very unsecure," in the machinery the splice cuts away. In the original text the "therefore" of the second sentence answers to a full chapter of motive-analysis. In the excerpt it answers to nothing. The passage presents as deduction what is actually a compressed history of reasons.

The ellipses, in other words, are not neutral. This work, as presented, is an argument whose premises live in the dots. Even the first clause originally ran "Men being, *as has been said*, by nature all free…" — an explicit flag that the axiom is inherited from earlier chapters, not established here. The splice converts a mid-system recapitulation into a self-grounding syllogism. That is rhetorically brilliant and logically circular: the certainty the passage radiates is borrowed certainty.

## Load-bearing words

Four words deserve weight.

*"Can."* Not "may not" — "no one *can* be subjected." The modal converts a moral prohibition into a quasi-natural impossibility: subjection without consent is not wrong but void, an artifact rather than a fact. This is rhetorically enormous and quietly question-begging; men are subjected without consent constantly, and the sentence's force depends on refusing to call that subjection. The same move governs the whole theory: it does not deny tyranny; it denies that tyranny is *power*.

*"Great and chief."* Not "only." The superlative ranks ends without closing the list — which simultaneously limits government (its chief purpose is set by the governed) and licenses it (subordinate purposes, punishment, war, adjudication, acquire derivative warrant). It is the hedge that makes the passage both a leash and an escape hatch, and later readers will pull on either end.

*"Property."* The elided ambiguity is the passage's most consequential feature. In Locke's own gloss — the sentence immediately preceding the splice — "property" is used "in a general sense" for "lives, liberties and estates." Read narrowly, government is an estate-manager's charter: an instrument of the propertied, and the classic critique (Macpherson's possessive individualism) sticks — the machinery protects stakeholders and quietly excludes servants, the propertyless, women, the colonized, who in Locke's own world were among the "men" not effectively consulted. Read broadly, the same sentence becomes the charter of the modern liberal state, entitled to regulate, tax, and redistribute in the name of preserving lives and liberties. The excerpt, by cutting the definition, permits both readings — which is perhaps why the excerpt circulates.

*"Preservation."* This is the only telos a liberal politics can share with everyone: it asks no citizen to adopt a conception of the good, only to keep what is already his. That neutrality is the passage's genius. It is also its emptiness. "Preservation" cannot settle what preservation *requires* when the protected goods conflict — when preserving lives demands taking property (taxation), a case Locke handles by straining his own possessive premise and appealing to consent-through-representation. An end that ranks but does not specify is a leash any strong hand can claim to hold for the right reasons.

## Where it strains

Hume's objection is historical and it lands: men are born into governments; no polity in record was founded by contract; the state of nature is a fiction with no counterpart. Bentham's objection is sharper and lands harder: natural rights as described here are claims for which no proof is offered and none can be — assertions dressed as axioms. Within the work's own method, neither can be answered, because the method forbids the evidence that would answer them.

Internally, the deepest strain is the consent it rests on. Express consent is too rare to found actual governments, so Locke falls back on tacit consent — walking a highway, holding land under a jurisdiction — which dissolves the doctrine into mere fact: anyone born anywhere "consents." And if consent must be counted, the machinery needs majority rule, which needs an antecedent unanimous compact binding dissenters to others' votes — a two-stage construction nowhere visible in the work's singular "his own consent."

Finally, the fiduciary model raises the question it cannot answer within itself: *who judges when the trustee betrays the trust?* Locke's answer — "the people shall be judge" — is either the completion of the theory or its unsolved center, depending on whether one is winning. The machinery ends where it began: in the state of nature, with force as the only remaining arbiter, which the tradition euphemized as an "appeal to heaven." A doctrine that makes revolution legitimate cannot make it orderly, and this work does not try.

What it misses, in its own terms: those who cannot consent (children, and, by extension, future generations bound by a founder's bargain they never struck — Jefferson's "earth belongs to the living" is the honest radicalization of this premise against Locke's own constitution); and disagreement itself. The consent machine presupposes agreement on the end it is built to serve. Modern politics is precisely the fight over what "preservation of their property" demands. The work founds government on a consensus of purpose that governments actually exist to adjudicate the absence of.

## What it assumes without saying

Seven assumptions, none argued in the work: that nature legislates (the normativity of the first clause); that individuals pre-exist their societies — "men's uniting into commonwealths" imagines complete selves prior to union, the fiction against which every communitarian critique since has aimed; that rights are possessions, even life and liberty figured as property — an idiom doing enormous work, since possessions can be held against the world and deposited with a trustee, which is exactly the fiduciary leverage; that will is the only alienating instrument, so force can never title; that consent can be aggregated and presumed; that the inconveniences of the state of nature supply the motive for exit; and that "men" names everyone — a universality the historical Locke did not honor in practice.

## Verdict

As demonstration, the work fails: its first premise is asserted, its "therefore" spans three unshown ladders, its key term is left ambiguous, and its central question — who judges breach of trust — is answered only by returning to the violence it was built to avoid. As an act of definition, it is epochal. It fixed the terms on which legitimacy could thereafter be argued — consent, end, trust, reversion — and thereby made absolute power arguable down to its foundations. The passage is a machine for converting a moral anthropology into a constitutional limit, and its true engine is a possessive pronoun: "their." Government preserves what it never owned; therefore it serves; therefore it can be judged; therefore, when it betrays, it reverts. Everything contested in liberal politics since — what counts as property, who consents, who judges — is the working out of what these three sentences compressed and their ellipses concealed.
