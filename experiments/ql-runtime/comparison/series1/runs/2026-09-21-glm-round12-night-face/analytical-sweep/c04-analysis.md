"Society is indeed a contract." The whole argument is folded into "indeed." Burke concedes the very term his opponents had made central — the contract, the voluntary bond, the agreement of the living — not to contest it but to raise its stakes. This is the passage's deep mechanism: redefinition under a retained word. The next clause refuses the diminished version by a double negative, and the refusal is worth reading slowly: the state "ought not to be considered as nothing better than a partnership agreement in a trade of pepper and coffee." The "ought" governs *consideration* before it governs conduct. Burke is legislating a manner of seeing — forbidding a certain regard — before he asserts anything about what the state is. And note the slide between the first sentence and the second: "society" is a contract affirmed; "the state" is a contract hedged. The softer, larger term gets the concession; the harder one gets the prohibition.

Why pepper and coffee? The mock specificity does the argument's work. Both goods are consumables: enjoyed without residue, exhausted in use, purchasable by weight. A partnership in consumables has a natural term — it lasts as long as the appetite. Against this Burke sets goods of a different kind: "a partnership in all science; a partnership in all art; a partnership in every virtue." Science, art, virtue are accumulables; no generation completes them, each inherits and extends them, and no single lifetime can even receive them whole. Here is the passage's genuine seed of reasoning: the object of a partnership determines its duration. Ends beyond a lifetime require a partnership beyond a lifetime. In Burke's original text this inference is explicit — "As the ends of such a partnership cannot be obtained in many generations, it becomes a partnership…" — and the excerpt cuts it. All three ellipses are editorial decisions with consequences: the first removes Burke's allowance that "subordinate contracts" may be dissolved at pleasure, a real concession to revolution in the small; the second removes the goods' characterization as "some other such low concern, to be taken up for a little temporary interest, and to be dissolved by the fancy of the parties," along with the instruction to look on the state "with other reverence"; the third removes "and in all perfection" and the inference itself. The excerpt converts derivation into fiat. It is not Burke's argument; it is Burke's argument compressed into aphorism — which is the form in which it has lived, quoted as settled wisdom rather than weighed as a move in a quarrel.

The rhetoric deserves attention because it is the argument. The tricolon of "a partnership in" — science, art, virtue — climbs from the epistemic through the aesthetic to the moral, and the anaphora enacts what it describes: each clause receives the previous one and adds to it, the sentence itself accumulating like an inheritance. The climax is not God but time: "not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." The repetition of "those who are living" is deliberate — the living are named twice, included but demoted from sole partners to one term among three. The grammar distributes the tenses: "are living" is an act in progress, "are dead" an act completed, "are to be born" an act promised. And the order matters — the dead come before the unborn, memory outranking anticipation. Two generations would make a history; the third term converts inheritance into debt.

Where the claim holds is as description rather than prescription. The goods named really are transgenerational; nobody now alive made their language, their mathematics, their law, and any actual partnership in science already includes the dead as silent collaborators. Read this way, Burke's sentence is less a political doctrine than an account of how cultural goods exist at all: only by transmission. The corollary is strong too. An entity whose end is the preservation of these goods cannot be wound up like a venture, because dissolving the state destroys not a provider of the goods but their medium — the state as container, not vendor. A trading partnership can be dissolved with its commodity intact; dissolve the polity and the partnership in science goes with it. That is the best argument the passage gestures at, and the excerpt leaves it implicit.

Where it strains is at the word "contract." A contract binds because parties consent and may withdraw. The dead cannot consent and cannot be released; the unborn cannot consent at all. Either the term is metaphor — in which case its normative engine, obligation by consent, has been silently replaced by obligation by inheritance and piety, the very things contract theory was invented to make unnecessary — or the term is literal, in which case the living are bound by terms they never agreed to and cannot renegotiate. The rejoinder writes itself, and was written: Paine called this governing beyond the grave. The passage's force lives in the gap between the word and its stretched meaning — it borrows the honorific of mutual agreement while abolishing mutuality.

The triad conceals a second assumption: it is coherent only if "the state" names a single continuing party, a corporate person persisting through successions of members. But that continuity is precisely what is in dispute between Burke and the revolutionaries. The passage assumes the intergenerational subject in order to derive obligations running between its members across time; the conclusion is disguised as a premise. A third assumption rides with it: that "science," "art," "virtue" pick out the same ends across centuries, so that one contract can cover the dead's pursuits and the living's. The partnership inherits the dead's errors with their achievements — Burke, who defended inherited prejudice as stored wisdom elsewhere, would accept this happily, but the excerpt never argues it, and the univocity of the tricolon does the quiet work.

Then there is the problem of power. In a partnership whose majority of parties are voiceless, whoever governs speaks for two of the three. The dead cannot correct the government's interpretation of their will; the unborn are the most pliable constituency in politics, invocable by every project from conservation to conquest. The mandate is unaccountable by design. And the excerpt provides no mechanism anywhere for amendment or adjudication: the standard — "every virtue" — floats above the state with no institution named to judge by it. In the full text the standard is theological, the partnership a clause in a "primæval contract" reaching "the invisible world"; cut here, the sanctity survives only as residue in the totality of "all… all… every" and the awe of the triad.

What the work assumes without saying, finally, is a whole moral world. That inheritance confers obligation — the passage runs on gratitude to ancestors as an unexamined sentiment. That commerce is base — the sneer at pepper and coffee is positioning, written in the capital of a commercial empire by a writer who had spent years showing what violence lay behind exactly those commodities. That civilization and the state are one thing — a partnership in science might in principle reside in the republic of letters, the church, the university; Burke assigns all of it to the state, the maximal claim. And that the generations harmonize in their ends.

The gloss that the present "holds the order in trust, not as owners" is therefore a domestication. The text says partnership *between* the living, the dead, and the unborn; a trust would make the living fiduciaries for principals located elsewhere, but Burke's stranger, stronger claim is that the principals include the nonexistent. What follows trustee-like conduct is a consequence, not the claim: the living, uniquely conscious, are the only partners who can act, and so the only ones who can keep or squander. The passage endures because its central term is stretched rather than demonstrated — which means it can be re-stretched by anyone, and has been, by traditionalists and sustainability politics alike, each quoting it against the other's pepper and coffee. It holds as a description of how cultural goods exist; it fails as a derivation of political obligation; its genius is to make the description do the derivation's work, beginning in the smallest possible place — the word "indeed."
