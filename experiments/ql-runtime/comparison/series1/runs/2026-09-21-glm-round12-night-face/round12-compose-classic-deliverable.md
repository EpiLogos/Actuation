# The Bond of Need

I read politics from a particular vantage, and I name it as mine: I do not see society as an agreement among strangers but as entanglement among the needy. No one chose their society, no one signed, and no two members agree on the good. If cohesion required consensus, none would exist. What exists instead is mutual dependence: each of us lives by what others do — grow food, treat illness, keep grids running and promises kept. This is the bond. It is neither sentimental nor contractual; it is the condition of being alive among others.

But dependence alone is not yet politics. It becomes political when it can be weaponised — when some acquire the standing to withhold what others cannot do without: income, protection, recognition, medicine. At that moment dependence curdles into domination, and the question of government begins.

What should govern, then, is not the majority's will as such, and not the market's verdict, but a discipline of non-domination: institutions whose continuing test is whether anyone's hold on another's necessities is being checked. This is what justifies democracy — not that voters are wise, but that elections are the one mechanism by which the dependent can answer back, replacing those who hold power over them. It is what justifies rule of law — not that statutes are noble, but that law binds the strong's discretion, the chief instrument of domination. Courts, welfare, unions, due process: each is, on this reading, the same thing done at different points — a hand loosening a grip.

An objection: this presumes conflict and underrates solidarity. Communities also cohere through love, shared meaning, sacrifice. True — but solidarity is the fruit of arrangements, not their foundation. A society cannot be founded on virtue, because virtue fails precisely when power is greatest; guarantees against domination, by contrast, reliably produce the trust from which solidarity grows. Order the state by the worst case, and you make room for the best.

So: we are bound by need, endangered by another's grip, and governed — rightly — by whatever keeps every grip open to challenge. That is the whole: one bond, one danger, one task.
