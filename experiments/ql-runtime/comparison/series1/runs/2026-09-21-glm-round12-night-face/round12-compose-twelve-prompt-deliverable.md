# The Republic of Kept Promises

What binds a society together is not likeness and not consent. Members do not choose one another as friends choose, nor do they share creed, origin, or aim. The bond is reliance: the settled expectation that strangers will honour what their offices, their contracts, and their given word commit them to. A society is a web of promises too large for any member to have made, and it stands or falls by whether the promises keep.

From this one claim the rest follows. Law is neither the command of the stronger nor the voice of a general will; it is the public form of the promise — the standing commitment by which a people binds its future selves. Institutions, then, are not neutral machinery but custodians of reliability, and they are to be judged only as custodians: does this court, this market, this school make commitments easier or harder to keep?

What should govern, therefore, is neither the majority's moment nor the ruler's wisdom, but the constitution in the older and larger sense: the arrangement that keeps the capacity to promise alive across generations. Three commitments follow, and I make them plainly.

First, legitimacy belongs to procedures that can fairly bind people not yet present: amendment possible but deliberate, rights stable but not untouchable, offices that outlast their holders.

Second, power is measured by a single test — whether it makes promises more or less keepable. On this account corruption is not merely unfair; it is solvent. Every favour that bends an office dissolves a portion of the bond everyone else is asked to honour.

Third, no identity claim, sacred or popular, overrides the standing commitments of the whole. A society that excuses itself from its own promises to some members has begun to come apart everywhere; exclusion at one point is default at all.

The objection is obvious: does this entomb the living beneath the past? It does not, because the governing promise is not any particular settlement but the practice of settlement itself. A revolution that renews reliability is faithful to the constitution even while overturning it; a seizure that merely replaces who benefits is not.

What binds us is reliance, sustained by institutions. What should govern is the kept promise, made constitutional. Markets, majorities, and traditions are instruments of that one end — or solvents of it.
